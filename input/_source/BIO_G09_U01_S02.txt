# Unit Overview



## Definition

The four threads of this unit: what biology is, the scientific method, lab skills and the microscope.



## Lead

Unit 1: Introduction to Biology serves as the foundational gateway to the entire Grade 9 Biology curriculum. This unit is designed to establish a solid conceptual framework for all subsequent biological study by introducing students to the fundamental nature of biology as a scientific discipline, the reasons why the study of life is important, the systematic methods biologists use to investigate the living world, the essential tools employed in biological research, and the critical safety practices that must be observed in any laboratory setting. The unit is organised around six interconnected sub-topics that progress logically from broad conceptual understanding to practical application. Students begin with the definition and scope of biology (Sub-topic 1.1), move through the importance of studying biology (1.2), learn the scientific method as it applies to biological problems (1.3), explore the diverse tools used by biologists in both laboratory and field settings (1.4), gain hands-on familiarity with the light microscope as an essential instrument of biological investigation (1.5), and conclude with the fundamental principles of laboratory safety (1.6).



## Paragraphs

The six sub-topics covered in this unit are carefully sequenced to build knowledge cumulatively. Sub-topic 1.1 (Definition of Biology) explores the etymological roots of the word biology from the Greek bios (life) and logos (study) and introduces the key properties that distinguish living from non-living matter, including cellular organisation, growth and development, reproduction, genetic information transmission, energy utilisation, homeostasis, and evolutionary adaptation. Sub-topic 1.2 (Why Study Biology?) examines the practical applications of biological knowledge in medicine, agriculture, conservation, food processing, and environmental protection, with particular emphasis on Ethiopian examples such as the improvement of teff (Eragrostis tef) yields, research into coffee (Coffea arabica) disease resistance, and the propagation of enset (Ensete ventricosum), also known as the false banana, which is a staple food crop for millions of Ethiopians in the southern highlands. Sub-topic 1.3 (The Scientific Method) provides a step-by-step introduction to the process of scientific inquiry, from observation and question formulation through hypothesis development, prediction, experimentation, data analysis, and conclusion drawing, with special attention to the design of controlled experiments and the role of variables. Sub-topic 1.4 (Tools of a Biologist) surveys the essential equipment used in biological investigation, ranging from basic laboratory glassware such as beakers, flasks, and test tubes to specialised instruments including microscopes and dissecting tools, as well as field equipment like hand lenses, insect nets, plant presses, and GPS devices. Sub-topic 1.5 (The Light Microscope) offers a detailed examination of the parts and proper use of this indispensable instrument, including how to calculate total magnification using the eyepiece and objective lens values. Sub-topic 1.6 (Laboratory Safety) covers the essential rules and procedures that ensure a safe working environment in any biology laboratory.

This unit is designed to be delivered over a period of ten to twelve class periods, each of approximately forty minutes in duration, though teachers may adjust the pace according to the needs of their students and the availability of laboratory resources. The unit assumes no prior formal study of biology, though students are expected to have a basic foundational knowledge of general science as acquired in earlier grades. Specifically, students should be familiar with the concept of scientific inquiry from their general science education and should have basic literacy and numeracy skills sufficient to read and understand scientific texts, record observations, and perform simple calculations such as multiplication for magnification problems. The unit is designed to be accessible to all Grade 9 students regardless of their previous exposure to biology, and it provides numerous opportunities for students to connect abstract scientific concepts to their own experiences and observations of the living world around them.

As the first unit of the Grade 9 Biology curriculum, this introduction plays a critical role in setting the stage for the more specialised topics that follow. Later units will build upon the foundational concepts established here, exploring cell biology (Unit 2), human biology and health (Unit 3), genetics (Unit 4), ecology and conservation (Unit 5), and the diversity of living organisms (Unit 6). The scientific method introduced in this unit will be applied repeatedly throughout the year as students design and conduct investigations, analyse data, and draw evidence-based conclusions. The microscope skills introduced here will be essential for the study of cells and microorganisms in subsequent units. Likewise, the laboratory safety practices established in this unit will govern all practical work throughout the year. Mastery of the concepts and skills presented in Unit 1 is therefore essential for success in the remainder of the Grade 9 Biology course and for the development of a deeper understanding of the living world.



## Ethiopian context

This unit is particularly relevant to Ethiopian students because it provides the scientific vocabulary and conceptual tools needed to understand and appreciate the extraordinary biological heritage of their own country. Ethiopia is one of the eight major centres of genetic diversity in the world for crop plants, and it is the centre of origin for coffee (Coffea arabica), one of the most economically important crops globally. Understanding the basic principles of biology introduced in this unit will enable students to engage more meaningfully with Ethiopia's biodiversity conservation efforts, including the work of Addis Ababa University's Department of Biology and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) in studying and protecting the nation's living resources. The unit also lays the groundwork for understanding the ecological significance of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) on the Blue Nile River and its implications for aquatic ecosystems and agricultural systems in the Nile Basin.
