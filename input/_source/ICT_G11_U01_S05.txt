# Section 5: 2. Introduction to Information Systems



## Definition

The central system depends on people, procedures, data, software, and hardware working together.



## Lead

An information system (IS) is a formally defined arrangement of people, hardware, software, data, and procedures that work together to collect, process, store, manage, and distribute information. Its purpose is to support decision-making, coordination, and control within an organisation. An IS is not merely a computer or piece of software — it is an integrated socio-technical system that combines technological components with human roles and organisational procedures.



## Paragraphs

Hardware refers to all the physical devices that make up the system: computers, servers, storage devices, network equipment, input devices (keyboards, scanners), and output devices (printers, monitors). In many parts of Ethiopia, hardware constraints — unreliable power supply, limited internet bandwidth, and the cost of equipment — remain a significant challenge to IS implementation.

Software encompasses all the programs, applications, and operating systems that direct the hardware to perform specific tasks. System software (operating systems like Windows or Linux) provides the platform on which application software runs. Many Ethiopian government organisations have moved to open-source software to reduce licensing costs.

Data is the raw material that the information system processes. Well-designed IS ensure that data is accurate, complete, consistent, timely, and relevant — qualities collectively referred to as data quality. Poor data quality is one of the most common causes of IS failure. If a hospital IS contains incorrect patient records, the decisions made using that information will be flawed regardless of how sophisticated the technology is.

People are perhaps the most important component of any IS, yet frequently underestimated. People play multiple roles: as users who interact with the system daily (cashiers, teachers, nurses), as administrators who maintain the system, and as analysts who design and improve it. They also serve as decision-makers who interpret its outputs. Ethiopia's e-government programmes have sometimes struggled precisely because insufficient attention was paid to training civil servants to use new systems effectively.

Procedures are the rules, policies, and step-by-step instructions that govern how the IS is used. Without clear procedures, even an excellent IS will be used inconsistently, producing unreliable outputs. Many cybersecurity breaches occur not because of technical vulnerabilities, but because procedures were not followed.

Ethiopia in Focus: The Human Resource Information System (HRIS) used by the Ethiopian Civil Service Commission manages personnel data for hundreds of thousands of federal civil servants. This system illustrates all five IS components in action: hardware (government servers and networked computers), software (the HRIS — IS application), data (employee records), people (HR officers and IT administrators), and procedures (standardised forms, data entry protocols, and access control policies).

Think Critically: Consider a small Ethiopian coffee cooperative that exports beans to European markets. What types of IS would be most useful at different levels of its operation? Think about: day-to-day recording of coffee purchases from farmers (TPS); monthly monitoring of export volumes and prices (MIS); the strategic decision about whether to seek organic certification (DSS); and long-term planning for market expansion (EIS). Now consider: which of the five IS components would be most difficult for this cooperative to put in place, given the realities of rural Ethiopia? Justify with specific reasoning.



## List items

Transaction Processing Systems (TPS) handle routine, day-to-day transactions — recording sales, processing payments, updating inventory. They process large volumes of transactions quickly and accurately and form the operational foundation of most organisations.

Management Information Systems (MIS) aggregate and summarise data from TPS — Transaction Processing Systems — to produce reports and dashboards for middle managers to monitor organisational performance and identify trends.

Decision Support Systems (DSS) use analytical models and "what-if" simulations to help senior managers evaluate complex decisions.

Executive Information Systems (EIS) provide strategic-level information in highly summarised formats for top executives, often incorporating external market data alongside internal data.
