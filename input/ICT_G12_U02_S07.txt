Key idea: The architecture shows the flow from sensing to communication, processing, analysis, and action.

Lesson content (authoritative — stay accurate and do not add facts beyond it):
The Internet of Things refers to the network of physical objects, or "things," that are embedded with sensors, software, and other technologies to connect and exchange data with other devices and systems over the internet. The architecture of an IoT system can be understood as a layered structure comprising four fundamental components. The perception layer consists of sensors and actuators that interact with the physical environment; sensors collect data such as temperature, humidity, motion, or light levels, while actuators perform physical actions such as opening a valve or switching on a motor. The network layer transmits data from sensors to processing systems using communication protocols such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee, LoRaWAN, or cellular networks.

By 2025, it is estimated that there will be more than 30 billion IoT-connected devices worldwide, generating over 79 zettabytes of data annually. In Ethiopia, the expansion of the 4G network and the impending rollout of 5G technology are expected to accelerate IoT adoption across agriculture, healthcare, transportation, and manufacturing sectors.

In the healthcare sector, IoT is enabling remote patient monitoring, telemedicine, and improved healthcare delivery in both urban and rural settings. IoT-enabled medical devices can continuously monitor patient vital signs such as heart rate, blood pressure, and blood glucose levels, transmitting data to healthcare providers who can intervene when abnormalities are detected.

Ethiopian cities, particularly Addis Ababa, are exploring smart city applications of IoT to improve urban services and quality of life. Smart traffic management systems using IoT sensors and cameras can monitor traffic flow in real time, adjusting traffic signal timing to reduce congestion and emissions.

IoT technologies are beginning to transform this sector through precision agriculture applications. Soil moisture sensors deployed across farm fields can transmit real-time data to farmers via mobile applications, enabling them to optimise irrigation schedules and conserve water resources. Weather stations equipped with IoT sensors provide hyperlocal temperature, rainfall, and wind speed data that inform planting and harvesting decisions. Livestock monitoring systems using GPS-enabled collars and health sensors allow pastoralist communities to track the location, movement patterns, and health status of their herds, reducing losses from disease and theft.
